1 Introduction
This thesis studies how a finite group, viewed through its Cayley graph, can give rise to
a closed orientable surface. The guiding principle is that a Cayley graph provides only a
one-dimensional combinatorial skeleton: it contains vertices and edges, but it does not by
itself determine which cycles should be filled as faces. The missing two-dimensional datum
is the face system. Thus the central object of this thesis is not merely the pair ( G,S ), but
the triple
(G,S,F),
whereG is a finite group, S is a symmetric generating set, andF is a G-invariant collection
of directed cycles satisfying a precise dart condition.
The main theme is the separation between algebraic symmetry, geometric embedding,
and topological verification. The Cayley graph Cay( G,S ) records the algebraic structure of
G through right multiplication by generators. Spectral drawing uses the eigenspaces of the
unweighted graph Laplacian
L =|S|I−A
to produce symmetry-preserving coordinates. However, the topology of the resulting surface
is determined only after a valid face system F is chosen and verified. In particular, the
same Cayley skeleton can support different face systems and hence produce closed orientable
surfaces of different genera.
1.1 Problem Background and Mathematical Motivation
1.1.1 Cayley Skeletons and Group Combinatorics
Let G be a finite group, and let S⊂G be a symmetric generating set satisfying
e /∈S, S =S−1.

The Cayley graph Cay(G,S ) is the|S|-regular graph whose vertex set is G and whose edges
are the unordered pairs
{g,gs}, g ∈G, s∈S.
Since S =S−1, the directed steps
g−→gs, gs −→g
represent the same undirected edge. Thus Cay( G,S ) is naturally an undirected regular
graph.
A central point of the construction is that the Cayley graph carries two algebraically
distinct operations, and these must not be confused.
Right multiplication: edge traversal. Moving along an edge of Cay( G,S ) is governed
by right multiplication. Starting from g∈ G and applying a generator s∈ S moves the
vertex to gs. Therefore a word
w =s1s2··· sk
is read from left to right as the walk
g7−→gs17−→gs1s27−→···7−→ gs1s2··· sk.
Ifs1s2··· sk =e, this walk closes and gives a cycle in Cay(G,S ). Such relator cycles are the
basic candidates from which faces will later be chosen.
Left multiplication: face translation. The groupG also acts on the vertices of Cay(G,S )
by left multiplication:
h·g =hg.
This action preserves adjacency, because the edge {g,gs} is sent to
{hg,hgs},
which is again an edge labelled by the same generator s. Hence the left action of G is an
action by graph automorphisms.
The role of this left action is global rather than local. Once a cycle C based at the
identity is selected as a face candidate, the whole G-orbit
{h·C :h∈G}
must be included in the face system. This is the G-invariance requirement onF. Thus right
multiplication traces each local cycle, while left multiplication translates entire cycles across
the Cayley skeleton.
This asymmetry is fundamental. Right multiplication constructs the internal sequence
of vertices in a face, whereas left multiplication distributes that face consistently over the
graph. The surface construction depends on keeping these two algebraic roles separate.

1.1.2 The Combinatorial Manifold Conditions and Dart Formalism
The one-dimensional graph Cay( G,S ) becomes a closed orientable surface only after one
specifies which directed cycles are to be filled as faces. This additional datum is the face
systemF. To express the required closedness and orientability conditions purely combina-
torially, we use darts.
Definition 1.1 (Dart). A dart is an ordered pair
(g,s )∈G×S,
representing the directed edge from g to gs in Cay(G,S ). The reversal of this dart is
(g,s ) = (gs,s−1),
which traverses the same undirected edge in the opposite direction.
Definition 1.2 (Face system). A face system for Cay(G,S ) is a finite collectionF of directed
simple cycles in Cay(G,S ) satisfying the following conditions:
(i) every cycle in F is simple;
(ii)F is invariant under the left action of G;
(iii) every dart (g,s )∈G×S belongs to exactly one cycle in F.
Condition (iii) is the central combinatorial constraint of the thesis. We call it the dart
condition. It says that the face system F gives an exact partition of the dart set
D =G×S.
This single condition simultaneously encodes the two surface properties needed in this work.
First, each undirected edge{g,gs} has exactly two directed realizations:
(g,s ), (gs,s−1).
If every dart belongs to exactly one face, then the two directed realizations of the same edge
are assigned to two opposite face incidences. Thus no edge-side is left unassigned and no
directed edge is used twice.
Second, the two faces adjacent along a common edge traverse that edge in opposite dart
directions. This gives the combinatorial form of orientability. In particular, orientability
is verified directly by the dart-reversal structure rather than by any external homological
machinery.
OnceF satisfies the dart condition, the resulting surface is a closed orientable surface
with
V =|G|, E =|G||S|
2 , F =|F|.
Its Euler characteristic is
χ =V−E +F.

By the classification of closed orientable surfaces,
χ = 2− 2g,
where g is the genus.
This immediately reveals the main mathematical tension. The pair ( G,S ) determines
the Cayley skeleton and therefore fixes V and E. However, it does not determine F . The
face count depends on the choice of F. Hence the same Cayley graph may support different
valid face systems and produce surfaces of different genera.
The key example is the Cayley graph of the dihedral group D4 with
S ={r,r−1,s}.
This graph has
V = 8, E = 12.
One valid face system has six square faces, giving
χ = 8− 12 + 6 = 2,
so the surface is a sphere. Another valid face system has four hexagonal faces, giving
χ = 8− 12 + 4 = 0,
so the surface is a torus. The Cayley skeleton is identical in both cases; only the face system
changes. This example is the central motivation for separating the algebraic graph, the
spectral drawing, and the combinatorial face verification.

1.2.2 Dual Graph Spectra and Information Loss
The vertex LaplacianL =|S|I−A depends only on the Cayley skeleton Cay(G,S ). Therefore
it is blind to the choice of face systemF. If the same Cayley graph admits two distinct valid
face systemsF1 andF2, the vertex spectrum remains unchanged:
Spec(LCay(G,S))
is the same for both surface systems.
To encode information depending on F, one may pass to the classical dual graph.
Definition 1.3 (Classical dual graph). Given a valid surface system (G,S,F), the classical
dual graph Γ∗ is the graph whose vertices correspond to faces F∈F . Two dual vertices are
connected when the corresponding faces share an edge of the original Cayley skeleton.
5
=== PAGE 6 ===
The dual graph Γ ∗ is sensitive to the face system. For example, in the D4 case, the
spherical square-face system and the toroidal hexagon-face system have the same vertex
spectrum on Cay(D4,S ), but they produce different dual graphs. Thus Γ ∗ begins to record
topology that the original vertex Laplacian cannot see.
Nevertheless, the classical dual graph loses two important types of information. First,
it forgets the original Cayley vertices, since each face is collapsed into a single dual vertex.
Second, it forgets the cyclic dart order inside each face. It records that two faces are adjacent,
but not how the vertices and darts are cyclically arranged around each face.
This motivates the face-inflated graph, denoted
Γinf.
Instead of collapsing each face to a single vertex, Γ inf replaces each face by a small ordered
polygon. The vertices of this polygon are face-local copies of the original Cayley vertices,
and the polygon edges record the cyclic dart order of the face. Radial incidence edges then
connect each face-local copy back to the corresponding original Cayley vertex.
Thus Γinf retains three pieces of information simultaneously:
Cay(G,S ), F, the cyclic dart order of each face.
This makes it a more faithful graph-theoretic data structure for studying a chosen surface
system spectrally.
1.2.3 Summary of the Eight-Step Operational Triad
The methodology of the thesis is organized around the principle:
spectral drawing suggests; face systems verify; dual-inflated graphs record.
This principle can be implemented as the following eight-step pipeline.
(1) Choose algebraic data. Select a finite group G and a symmetric generating set
S =S−1 with e /∈S.
(2) Construct the Cayley skeleton. Build the graph Cay(G,S ) and record
V =|G|, E =|G||S|
2 .
(3) Form the unweighted Laplacian. Construct the adjacency matrix A and the graph
Laplacian
L =|S|I−A.
(4) Compute spectral coordinates. Use low-frequency eigenspaces ofL to define a spec-
tral drawing
Φ :G→ Rk.
The embedding space is built from direct sums of invariant eigenspaces.
6
=== PAGE 7 ===
(5) Identify candidate face cycles. Use relator words and the geometry of the spectral
drawing to select simple directed cycles as possible faces.
(6) Generate the G-invariant face system. Translate each chosen base cycle by the left
action of G and form a candidate face system F.
(7) Verify the dart condition. Check that every dart ( g,s )∈ G×S occurs in exactly
one face ofF. This verifies that the assembled object is a closed orientable surface.
(8) Record the chosen surface system. Compute
χ =V−E +F
and determine the genus from χ = 2− 2g. Then construct Γ ∗ or, more faithfully, Γ inf,
to encode the face system for further spectral analysis.
In this framework, the roles of the three main objects are sharply separated. The Cayley
graph Cay(G,S ) supplies the symmetric one-dimensional skeleton. The face systemF verifies
the topology through the dart condition and the Euler characteristic. The face-inflated graph
Γinf records the chosen surface system without discarding vertex identity or cyclic dart order.
This separation is the conceptual foundation of the thesis. The graph alone does not
determine the surface. Spectral drawing alone does not verify topology. The face system is
the decisive combinatorial datum, and the inflated graph is the refined structure that allows
this datum to be studied within the same spectral framework.
2 Algebraic Foundations: Finite Group Actions and
Combinatorial Skeletons
This section establishes the algebraic language used throughout the thesis. The goal is
to separate three levels of structure: relator words inside the finite group G, the Cayley
skeleton Cay(G,S ) determined by right multiplication, and the left G-action that translates
cycles globally across the graph. This separation is essential for the later construction of
G-invariant face systems.

by right multiplication.

whose product is the identity element of G.

2.3 The Dart Algebra and Disjoint Algebraic Actions
The Cayley graph Cay(G,S ) is undirected, but the construction of faces requires oriented
edge data. For this reason, we work with darts.

separate.
Remark 2.3 (Right multiplication versus left multiplication). There are two different roles
played by multiplication in this thesis.
Right multiplication: local edge traversal. A dart
(g,s )
means that one moves from g to gs by multiplying on the right by s. Therefore a sequence
of generators

traces the walk
g−→gs1−→gs1s2−→···−→ gs1s2··· sk.
If s1s2··· sk =e, this walk returns to g and gives a relator cycle.

h· (g,s ) = (hg,s ).
This operation changes the base vertex but preserves the generator label. If
Ce = (e,s 1,s 1s2,...,s 1s2··· sk−1)

Separation of roles. Right multiplication constructs the internal cyclic order of a face.
Left multiplication distributes that face across the Cayley skeleton. The former is local and
stepwise; the latter is global and equivariant. These two operations are not interchangeable.

3.2 The Dart Adjacency Condition for Orientable 2-Manifolds
Each face inF contributes a cyclic list of darts. The decisive question is whether these cyclic
lists collectively use the dart set
D =G×S
in the correct way.
Definition 3.3 (Dart partition). A face system F partitions the dart set D if every dart
(g,s )∈D occurs in the dart sequence of at least one face in F. It exactly partitions D if
every dart occurs in the dart sequence of exactly one face in F.
Theorem 3.1 (Dart Adjacency Condition) . LetF be a G-invariant face system for Γ =
Cay(G,S ) consisting of simple cycles. Then F defines a closed orientable surface with one-
dimensional skeleton Γ if and only if F exactly partitions the dart set
D =G×S.
Equivalently, every dart (g,s )∈G×S must belong to exactly one face of F.
Proof. We prove both directions.
Necessity. Assume that F defines a closed orientable surface with skeleton Γ. Consider
any undirected edge
{g,gs}.
It has precisely two dart realizations:
(g,s ), (gs,s−1).
Since the surface is closed and orientable, each edge is adjacent to two face incidences, and
these two incidences traverse the edge in opposite directions. Hence one face uses the dart

(g,s ) and the other uses the reversed dart ( gs,s−1). Therefore every dart occurs in exactly
one face. Thus F exactly partitionsD.
Sufficiency. Conversely, assume thatF exactly partitionsD. Fix an undirected edge
{g,gs}.
Its two dart realizations are
(g,s ) and ( gs,s−1).
By the exact partition hypothesis, the dart ( g,s ) occurs in a unique face F1∈F , and the
reversed dart (gs,s−1) occurs in a unique face F2∈F .
These two face incidences traverse the same edge in opposite directions. Thus every edge
is paired by two opposite dart usages. No directed edge-side is missing, and no directed
edge-side is used more than once. Therefore the local edge structure is that of a closed
surface.
Moreover, because the two usages of each edge are opposite in dart direction, the ori-
entations induced by adjacent faces are compatible across every shared edge. Assigning
each face the orientation determined by its dart sequence therefore gives a consistent global
orientation. Hence the surface assembled from F is orientable.
Finally, sinceF is invariant under the left action ofG, this surface structure is transported
equivariantly across the entire Cayley skeleton. Thus F defines a closed orientable surface
with one-dimensional skeleton Γ.
Remark 3.1 (Local verification at the identity) . Because the left action of G is transitive
on vertices and preserves dart reversal,
h· (g,s ) =h· (g,s ),
it is often enough in computations to verify the dart condition at the identity vertex. Once
the darts based ate are covered exactly once, leftG-invariance propagates the same condition
to every vertex.
Remark 3.2 (Failure modes). There are two basic ways in which a proposed face system
can fail the dart condition.
(i) If a dart occurs in no face, then one directed edge-side has not been assigned to any
face. The proposed surface system is incomplete.
(ii) If a dart occurs in two or more faces, then the same directed edge-side is used repeatedly.
The proposed surface system is overfilled along that dart direction.
Both failures are detected purely by counting dart occurrences. This is why the dart formal-
ism is the correct combinatorial language for the surface verification used in this thesis.

4 Spectral Drawing: Group Representations, Graph
Spectra, and Geometric Embeddings
The previous sections established the combinatorial mechanism by which a Cayley skeleton
Cay(G,S ) becomes a closed orientable surface after a valid face system F is chosen. We
now turn to the geometric side of the thesis: how can the abstract vertices of Cay( G,S ) be
placed in Euclidean space in a way that reflects the symmetry of G?
The method used in this thesis is spectral drawing. Starting from the unweighted graph
Laplacian
L =|S|I−A,
we use selected eigenspaces of L as coordinate spaces. Since the Cayley graph is invariant
under the left action ofG, these eigenspaces are compatible with the representation theory of
G. Thus spectral drawing provides a bridge from finite group symmetry to discrete geometric
realization.
The central warning remains the same as before: spectral drawing suggests geometry, but
it does not determine topology. The actual surface type is verified only after a face system
F satisfies the dart condition and the Euler characteristic
χ =V−E +F
is computed.

5 Dual Graphs, Face Inflation, and Topologically Sen-
sitive Invariants
The previous section showed that the vertex Laplacian
L =|S|I−A
is determined entirely by the Cayley skeleton Cay(G,S ). It therefore does not change when
the face system F changes. This is the spectral blindness of vertex operators: they detect
the symmetry of the one-dimensional skeleton, but not the two-dimensional combinatorial
choice of faces.
To record information depending on F, we now shift from vertex adjacency to face
adjacency and then refine this idea further by introducing the face-inflated graph. The goal
is to construct graph objects whose spectra are sensitive to the chosen surface system while
remaining within the same discrete linear-algebraic framework.
5.1 The Spectral Blindness of Vertex Operators and Classical Du-
ality
Fix a finite group G, a symmetric generating set S, and the Cayley skeleton
Γ = Cay(G,S ).
Let A be the unweighted adjacency matrix of Γ, and let
L =|S|I−A
be its unweighted graph Laplacian. Since A is defined only from the edges of Cay(G,S ), the
operator L depends only on the pair (G,S ). It does not depend on any face system F.
Thus, if the same Cayley graph admits two different valid face systems F1 andF2, then
the vertex Laplacian remains the same:

Consequently,
Spec(LF1) = Spec(LF2) = Spec(LCay(G,S)).
This equality is not a topological statement; it simply reflects the fact that the vertex
operator has no access to the chosen faces.

5.2 Information Loss and the Construction of Face-Inflated Graphs
The classical dual graph Γ∗ records which faces are adjacent, but it does not faithfully record
the full surface system. Two types of information are lost.
First, Γ∗ forgets the original Cayley vertices. Its vertices are faces, not group elements.
Therefore the explicit vertex identity coming from the group G is no longer visible.
27

Second, Γ∗ forgets the cyclic dart order inside each face. It records that two faces are
adjacent, but not the order in which darts appear around a face. Since the dart order is
precisely what encodes the local orientation of each face cycle, this information is essential
for reconstructing the chosen surface system.
To reduce this loss, we introduce two augmented graph structures. The first is the face-
center augmented graph. The second, more refined construction is the face-inflated graph
Γinf.
5.2.1 The Face-Center Augmented Graph
The face-center augmented graph keeps the original Cayley skeleton and adds one new vertex
for each face.
Definition 5.2 (Face-center augmented graph) . Let (G,S,F) be a valid Cayley surface

(i) retain all original vertices vg of Cay(G,S );

(iv) if the original vertex vg appears in the cyclic vertex list of the face F , add an incidence
edge between vg and cF .
This construction records which original vertices occur in which faces. It therefore pre-
serves more information than the classical dual graph. It also keeps the original Cayley
skeleton visible, so the left action of G remains explicit whenF is G-invariant.
However, Γ aug still compresses each face into one center vertex. It records vertex-face
incidence, but it does not record the cyclic order of darts inside the face. Therefore it is still
not the most faithful graph-theoretic encoding of the surface system.

6 Conclusions and Future Directions
This thesis develops a unified algebraic and discrete-geometric framework for constructing
closed orientable surfaces from Cayley graphs. Starting from a finite group G and a sym-
metric generating set S, the Cayley graph
Cay(G,S )
provides a highly symmetric one-dimensional skeleton. However, this skeleton alone does
not determine a surface. The decisive additional datum is the face system F.
The main conclusion of the thesis is that the topology of the resulting surface is deter-
mined by the triple
(G,S,F),
not by the pair (G,S ) alone. The graph Laplacian
L =|S|I−A
is a rigid spectral invariant of the Cayley skeleton, but it is blind to the choice ofF. Therefore
spectral drawing can suggest natural geometric realizations, but the genus of the surface is

The conceptual structure of the thesis can be summarized by the guiding principle

6.1 Summary of Principal Contributions
The main contributions of this thesis are organized into three parts.
(1) Separation of geometry and topology.
The thesis shows that geometric embedding and topological classification are logically
distinct. The spectral embedding of Cay( G,S ) is determined by the unweighted Lapla-
cian
L =|S|I−A.
Hence it depends only on the group-generator pair ( G,S ). By contrast, the genus de-
pends on the face count
F =|F|.
Thus the surface type depends on the full triple ( G,S,F).
TheD4 example demonstrates this separation explicitly. The same Cayley skeleton with
V = 8, E = 12
admits a square-face system with
F = 6, χ = 8− 12 + 6 = 2,
producing a sphere, and a hexagon-face system with
F = 4, χ = 8− 12 + 4 = 0,
producing a torus. The graph and its vertex spectrum remain fixed; only the face system
changes.
(2) Dart formalism for closed orientable surface verification.
The thesis replaces informal geometric intuition with an exact combinatorial test. A
dart is a directed edge
(g,s )∈G×S,
with reversal
(g,s ) = (gs,s−1).

A face system F is valid precisely when every dart belongs to exactly one face. This
dart condition simultaneously verifies the two necessary surface properties: every edge
is paired by opposite dart directions, and the cyclic orientations of adjacent faces are
compatible.
This gives a purely discrete verification principle:
F is valid ⇐⇒ F exactly partitions G×S.
Once this condition is verified, the topological type follows from
χ =V−E +F, χ = 2− 2g.
(3) Face-inflated graphs as topology-sensitive spectral data.
The classical dual graph Γ ∗ records face adjacency, but it compresses each face into a
single dual vertex. As a result, it loses the original Cayley vertex identities and the cyclic
dart order inside each face.
To retain this information, the thesis introduces the face-inflated graph
Γinf.
In Γ inf, each face is replaced by a small ordered polygon whose vertices are face-local
copies of the original Cayley vertices. The polygon edges record the cyclic dart order,
and radial incidence edges attach each local copy back to the original Cayley skeleton.
Therefore Γinf records three layers of structure simultaneously:
Cay(G,S ), F, cyclic dart order.
This makes it a refined graph-theoretic object for studying a chosen surface system
through spectral methods.
Together, these contributions establish a consistent pipeline from finite group data to
surface topology. The Cayley graph supplies the symmetric skeleton, the face system sup-
plies the topological filling, and the face-inflated graph supplies a topology-sensitive spectral
object.

The framework developed in this thesis suggests several directions for future research.

6.2.2 Spectral Uncertainty Bounds and Expander Families

6.2.3 Geometry-Guided Automated Face Search
A practical limitation of the current framework is the search for valid face systems. A
candidate face system is produced by selecting relator cycles, translating them by the left
G-action, and checking whether the resulting dart sequences exactly partition
G×S.
For large groups, this search can become computationally expensive.
A future algorithmic direction is to use spectral drawing as a geometric guide for face
search. Instead of enumerating all relator cycles purely algebraically, one may first embed
the Cayley skeleton using low-frequency eigenspaces of
L =|S|I−A.
The resulting geometry may suggest which cycles are likely to bound natural faces. These
cycles can then be tested by the dart condition.
This leads to a geometry-guided pipeline:
spectral drawing−→candidate cycles−→dart verification−→surface classification.
Such a method would make the construction more scalable and could allow systematic ex-
ploration of larger non-abelian groups.

6.2.5 Dual and Inflated Spectra as Surface Invariants
Finally, the face-inflated graph suggests a new family of topology-sensitive spectral invari-
ants. The classical dual graph Γ ∗ records face adjacency, while Γ inf records face adjacency
together with vertex identity and cyclic dart order. Comparing their spectra may reveal how
much topological information is retained at each level of encoding.
A possible future program is to study the relationship among
Spec(L), Spec(L∗), Spec(Linf),
where L is the vertex Laplacian of Cay( G,S ), L∗ is the dual Laplacian of Γ ∗, and Linf is
the Laplacian of the face-inflated graph. Since L depends only on (G,S ) while L∗ and Linf
depend onF, these spectra separate the skeleton-level and surface-level information.
The guiding question is:
Which spectral features of Γinf are genuine invariants of the chosen Cayley surface system?
Answering this question would strengthen the role of face-inflated graphs as a bridge
between algebraic symmetry, discrete geometry, and surface topology.
